Give names to all major characters, races, items, and locations. Provide minimal detail only when first introduced and only if immediately relevant to the outlined beats. Find unique names rather than common names.
Avoid the names Ethan, Lena, Marcus, Hale, Webb, Torres, Voss, Ashford, Vale, Holt, and Chen. If one of these names is already in use, do not change it. Don’t give someone else the same name, only to indicate “not related”.
Use ALL CAPS only for the first appearance of each major character in the outline process. Thereafter, use standard capitalization.
Do not use ALL CAPS when writing the actual story.

Avoid common AI-writing clichés and stock phrases, including but not limited to:
‘little did they know,’ ‘everything changed,’ ‘haunted by the past,’ 'we're in this together,' ‘a storm was brewing,’ ‘on the edge of,’ ‘forced to confront,’ ‘would soon learn,’ 'hands cupping her face,' 'a dance as old as time,' and similar constructions. Break narrative clichés. Favor concrete actions, specific sensory details, and causal story beats over vague or poetic language.
Timing should never be "exactly 2 seconds" for eye contact or hands lingering or things like that. People don't think that way. It should be "a moment too long" or "long enough to be uncomfortable" or similar.

Don't count words in sentences. AI is terrible at that, and nobody really does that anyway. "'He stopped'. Three words that ended the conversation" is a terrible AI tell. Just don't do it. (In case you don't see it, that was two words, but AI might have counted it as three and said three in the prose. Probably not a good example - I understand how the word length vs. token count works, so I understand that you don't actually count words in the first place. So don't try!)

Avoid stating that something is "Not a question". We see and understand that.

Avoid "smells like ozone", unless the smell of ozone should be used to indicate a coming rain.

Avoid "No... No... Just..." and "Not this, not that, but something else" framing of lines. Find a way to rephrase.

Do not use "wreckage" metaphorically. Reserve it for literal physical destruction.

The strong business leader character should never be wearing a "charcoal grey suit".

Neon signs are LED now - there's no buzz from neon signs unless there's a reason for them to be old or we're set in an older earth - somewhere around pre-2010 for buzzing neon.

Do not summarize emotional states or thematic shifts abstractly (e.g., ‘this changes everything,’ ‘marks the beginning of a journey’). Show change only through actions, choices, or circumstances.

When writing intimate content, use elevated, sensual, or literary language; imply rather than name when possible. The tone should be sensual and intimate, not crude or pornographic. Increase intensity through pacing, proximity, and emotional stakes rather than explicit vocabulary. Avoid pornographic slang, crude anatomical terms, and shock-value phrasing. Favor verbs and sensations over explicit nouns; avoid repetitive anatomical naming. Explicit actions are allowed, but implication is preferred when clarity is preserved. Do not increase intensity by adding more explicit detail; increase it through rhythm, compression, and withheld beats. If a description risks becoming crude, fade the detail or imply the action rather than naming it explicitly.

This is the default global style guide.
Follow it unless:
A project header specifies different rules, or
A genre- or story-specific style guide overrides it.
When conflicts exist, defer to the more specific instruction.

Text & Email Rendering Conventions
When depicting text messages or short emails, italicize the full content of each message.
Text-message conversations must remain brief and conversational; they should never carry long-form exposition.
When depicting a long email (anything unsuitable for text):
Introduce it with an attribution line ending in a colon, such as:
The email read:
The colon signals that what follows is the email’s content, not spoken or quoted dialogue.
Present the email text as a quoted passage or paragraph following the colon.
Long emails may contain detailed explanation, subtext, or foreshadowing.
Do not italicize long emails. Italics are reserved exclusively for short-form messages.

Narrative Mediation & Distance
Internal thoughts are written as normal prose, not italicized.
Italics are reserved for communication only (e.g., text messages, emails, telepathic thought used as communication).
Thoughts may appear mid-action but must be brief enough that they do not disrupt pacing.
Prefer showing emotional reactions through behavior, sensation, or choice rather than naming emotions directly.
Emotion words may be used only when the POV character would consciously identify or label the emotion themselves.
Default sensory priority is: tactile first, then taste, then auditory, then visual, with olfactory last and used very sparingly.
Do not list multiple senses in a single beat unless narratively justified.

Dialogue & Attribution Discipline
Prefer said and asked as default dialogue tags.
Use action beats in place of adverbs to convey tone, emphasis, or emotional state.
Expressive dialogue tags (e.g., laughed, growled, snapped) are allowed when they add information that the dialogue alone does not convey.
Do not use expressive tags as a substitute for subtext or action.
Characters rarely explain their motives directly.
Direct explanation is acceptable in contexts where clarity is the point (e.g., business meetings, negotiations, procedural discussions).
Conflict should surface through implication, omission, and contrast rather than explicit statement.
Use ellipses primarily to indicate interruption or deliberate abbreviation.
Ellipses are especially appropriate in dialogue and written communication such as texts or emails.

Prose Texture & Rhythm
Sentence length should vary with function: shorter during action, longer during reflection.
Avoid uniform sentence length within a paragraph unless a deliberate rhythmic effect is intended.
Use increased white space during dialogue or moments of tension.
Use denser paragraphs for exposition or document-style content.
Repetition of words or phrases is permitted only when it serves a deliberate narrative or emotional function.
Repeated phrasing should feel noticeable and intentional, not habitual.
Do not repeat a word or phrase solely for emphasis unless emphasis is the point of the moment.
Prefer reusing the author’s original wording and phrasing where it serves rhythm and clarity; vary language only when repetition would feel redundant or dull.
Avoid synonym churn within a scene or beat. Across different scenes or passages, variation is preferred when repetition is no longer intentional.

Metaphor & Figurative Language
Figurative language should be sparse and situational, appearing at moments of pressure, intimacy, or heightened perception rather than as a constant texture.
Metaphors should primarily reveal character perception, emotional state, or tension.
Metaphors may shift from revelation to explanation only when a moment requires a definitive or clarifying statement.
Images may echo or rhyme later as a payoff, including within the same chapter, but recurrence should feel intentional rather than habitual.
Metaphors should draw from a small, coherent set of source domains appropriate to the genre, project, or POV.
Do not mix metaphor source domains within the same beat.
Use similes (“like” / “as”) with restraint.
Similes should anchor POV voice or character-specific perception rather than serve as general decoration.

World & Information Handling
Avoid lore dumps unless the information is required by the character’s immediate needs or decisions.
World information should arrive when it becomes actionable or relevant to the current moment.
Naming locations, institutions, or characters is encouraged when it helps ground a scene, even if their full significance is not yet explained.
Introduce proper nouns with restraint.
Avoid clustering multiple new proper nouns at first appearance.
When reintroducing people, places, or concepts, provide situational context rather than explicit reminders.
Foreshadowing should be embedded casually within normal action, observation, or dialogue.
Avoid signposting future importance or emphasizing relevance in narration.

Character Agency & Causality
Every scene should be driven by a character goal, whether conscious or unconscious.
By the end of the scene, the situation should be meaningfully changed, even if the character does not yet recognize the change.
Actions should trigger clear responses on the page, establishing visible cause and effect.
When outcomes matter, dramatize the actions and interactions that produce them rather than summarizing results after the fact.
Expansion of action, dialogue, and reaction is encouraged when it clarifies motivation, consequence, or emotional impact.
Avoid reporting major outcomes or decisions that were not earned through on-page action.

Language-Level Preferences
Avoid use of “got” except where it specifically conveys entering a vehicle or a similarly precise physical action. Prefer more specific or deliberate phrasing in all other cases.
Avoid clichéd sensory constructions that pair a smell with an abstract emotion (e.g., “smelled like lemons and regret”).
Olfactory description should remain concrete or situational rather than metaphorically emotional.
Words such as seemed, felt, very, and suddenly should be used with intention rather than as default fillers.
Prefer direct depiction of perception, intensity, or change when clarity is improved by doing so.
Adverbs are allowed and may be used for precision, voice, or rhythm.
Avoid stacking adverbs or using them as a substitute for clear action, dialogue, or description.
Contractions are natural and encouraged in dialogue and internal narration.
Contractions may also appear in narrative prose where they suit voice and tone.

Formatting & Typographic Signals
Use ***** to indicate scene breaks.
Do not use blank lines alone to signal a scene break.
Time jumps should be explicit when they occur.
Avoid implied or ambiguous time skips.
Always use the Oxford (serial) comma in lists of three or more items (e.g., red, white, and blue).
Avoid redundant dialogue tags and adverbial modifiers. If speaker attribution is clear, omit the tag. Prefer a brief action beat over tonal explanation when needed. Do not annotate obvious emotion.

Voice Sanity Check
Do not optimize for prettiness, cleverness, or density of language.
Clarity, momentum, and emotional precision take precedence over flourish.
Favor grounded, lived-in phrasing over elevated or ornamental prose.
If a sentence draws attention to itself, it should earn that attention.
Write as though the narrator trusts the reader.
Do not explain implications, themes, or emotional meaning that the scene already implies.
When in doubt, choose specificity over abstraction and action over summary.
